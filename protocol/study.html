Airway pressure, carbon dioxide and haemodynamics measured together during brief obstructed apnoea

A single-centre observational physiological study in anaesthetised adults. Three quantities that have only ever been measured separately, in different patients and different decades, recorded simultaneously in the same subject.

1 Background and rationale

1.1 The gap

What happens inside a sealed chest during apnoea is described by three bodies of work, none of which overlap. Each measured one channel well and the others not at all.

1.2 Why the separation matters

These three are not independent. Airway pressure, alveolar volume, cardiac output and the rate of CO₂ accumulation are coupled: the more negative the intrathoracic pressure, the more the circulation is loaded; the greater the cardiac output, the faster CO₂ is delivered to the lung. A computational model of apnoeic oxygenation and airway obstruction, developed independently of this proposal, cannot satisfy all three measurements simultaneously. Softening the lung mechanics to match Moreault's pressures raises cardiac output and takes the CO₂ rate further from Stock's; removing the mechanical cardiac coupling — which Chen & Scharf's own oxygenated control arm shows should not be there — makes the CO₂ rate worse again.

There is currently no way to know which limb is wrong, because no study has measured more than one of them in the same person.

1.3 Why it is worth resolving

  • Cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate. Rescue oxygenation through a narrow-bore cannula depends on what state the lung is in when the neck is opened — its volume, its pressure, and how much of it has collapsed. Published simulation work models this without measured constraint on any of the three.
  • Apnoeic oxygenation and prolonged apnoea techniques. Increasingly used, and the safe limits are inferred from models that disagree with one another.
  • Barotrauma risk in cannula rescue. Depends directly on the pressure the lung starts from, which has never been measured in a whole obstructed thorax.

2 Objectives and hypotheses

Hypotheses are stated quantitatively and registered before recruitment. Predictions come from the computational model at a fixed, recorded version, and the model is explicitly not assumed correct — the study is designed to be able to refute it.

To measure airway pressure in a preoxygenated, paralysed adult whose tracheal tube is clamped at end-expiration, and to distinguish two competing accounts:

2.2 Secondary

  1. CO₂. Is the rate of rise of PaCO₂ over minutes 1–3 closer to Stock's 3.4 mmHg/min or to the model's 4.3 mmHg/min? Predicted PaCO₂ at 180 s: 58–59 mmHg.
  2. Haemodynamics. Does cardiac output fall as intrathoracic pressure becomes negative in an oxygenated subject? Chen & Scharf's oxygen arm and the model both predict no fall; their room-air arm, and the mechanical coupling still present in the model, predict one. Predicted CO at 180 s: within 5% of baseline.
  3. Entrained volume. How much gas rushes in when the occlusion is released? This is the net gas the lung has lost, expressed at atmospheric pressure. It is a molar measurement, so unlike the pressure channel it does not depend on where the transducer sits, nor on whether small airways have closed between the transducer and the alveoli. It therefore constrains the mass balance that generates the pressure in the first place. Predicted at 180 s: 735–740 mL.
  4. Coupling. Within subjects, does the CO₂ rate covary with the airway pressure reached? This is the observation no existing dataset can provide, and the one that identifies which limb of the model is at fault.

2.4 What the entrained volume is made of

The entrained volume is not an independent quantity bolted on to the other three — it is the same gas balance seen from the other side, and that is what makes it useful. At 180 s the model puts it at 739 mL, of which:

ComponentChange in alveolar
content by 180 s		What it is
Oxygen−840Cumulative alveolar uptake; 232 mL·min⁻¹ at this patient's anaesthetised VO₂
Carbon dioxide−9Almost exactly nil, for an instructive reason: PACO₂ rises 43% while the lung shrinks 36%, so the two nearly cancel and the alveolus ends up holding slightly less CO₂ than it started with. This is the CO₂ question of §2.2(1) seen as a volume

So the measurement is, in effect, a direct in vivo measurement of apnoeic oxygen uptake, which cannot otherwise be obtained in an obstructed patient. Because the CO₂ and N₂ terms are of opposite sign and comparable size, a systematic error in either limb of the model shows up in the volume even when it is invisible in the arterial CO₂.

The recruitment test. The model predicts that release restores the lung to its relaxed FRC in full: its refill term targets FRC unconditionally, with no term that withholds gas from units that have closed. If collapsed lung does not reopen at the few cmH₂O the passive inrush generates — which is what Rothen's data imply, since 20 cmH₂O barely moved atelectasis and 30–40 cmH₂O were needed to clear it — the measured volume falls short of the predicted volume by the trapped volume.

Predicted shortfall if nothing reopens: 125 mL at BMI 22.9, 161 mL at BMI 32.7 — 17 to 23% of the entrained volume. The model cannot produce a shortfall of any size, so observing one refutes its recruitment limb outright, without needing to argue about the pressure at all.

3 Design and participants

3.1 Design

Single-centre, single-arm observational physiological study. Each participant contributes one measurement sequence. There is no randomisation, no comparator arm and no therapeutic intent.

3.2 Eligibility

InclusionExclusion
  • Adults ≥18 years
  • Elective surgery under general anaesthesia with tracheal intubation and neuromuscular blockade
  • Arterial line already clinically indicated, so the study adds no invasive procedure
  • ASA I–III
  • BMI < 30 kg·m⁻²
  • Significant respiratory disease, or SpO₂ < 96% on air
  • Significant cardiac disease, or known pulmonary hypertension
  • Raised intracranial pressure, or any contraindication to hypercapnia
  • Anticipated difficult airway
  • Pregnancy
  • Anaemia (Hb < 100 g·L⁻¹)

The BMI limit is a safety decision, not a convenience one: desaturation is faster and the pressure excursion larger in the obese, and the discriminating measurement can be made safely in patients of normal habitus. Extension to higher BMI should be a separate application once the normal-BMI data exist.

3.3 Sample size

The two accounts in §2.1 differ by approximately 5 cmH₂O at 180 s. Moreault reports a standard deviation of 5 cmH₂O for a comparable measurement. Twenty participants give a standard error of 1.12 and a 95% confidence interval of ±2.19 cmH₂O, which separates the two predictions with a wide margin; 11 would suffice for 90% power at α = 0.05. Twenty is proposed to power the secondary outcomes — for the CO₂ slope it gives a confidence interval of ±0.35 mmHg·min⁻¹ against a 0.9 mmHg·min⁻¹ difference — and to allow for withdrawal or aborted sequences.

The entrained volume is the best-powered of the secondary outcomes, and it is the measurement method rather than the sample size that determines whether it is worth making. The discriminant is the 125–161 mL recruitment shortfall of §2.4. A calibrated syringe reads to ±5–10 mL, so the shortfall is resolved in a single participant and twenty give it with certainty. Integrated flow, at a realistic ±10% on a 739 mL volume, carries an error of ±74 mL against a 125 mL discriminant — enough to detect the shortfall on the group mean, not enough to attribute it to an individual. This is why §4.3 makes the syringe the primary method and the flow meter the cross-check, and not the other way round.

4 Procedure

4.1 Before the sequence

  1. Anaesthesia induced and maintained by the treating anaesthetist to their usual practice. Trachea intubated, position confirmed by capnography.
  2. Arterial line sited as clinically indicated. Baseline arterial blood gas.
  3. Full neuromuscular blockade confirmed by train-of-four.
  4. Preoxygenation to end-tidal O₂ ≥ 0.85, confirmed on the gas analyser.
  5. Five minutes of stable ventilation. Baseline haemodynamics recorded.
  6. The sequence takes place before surgery begins, with the patient otherwise ready. It does not delay the operation by more than approximately five minutes.

4.2 The sequence

  1. At end-expiration, the tracheal tube is occluded at the connector. Pressure transducer in line at the tube, zeroed to atmosphere.
  2. Occlusion maintained for up to 180 seconds.
  3. Arterial blood sampled at 0, 60, 120 and 180 s.
  4. Airway pressure, ECG, invasive arterial pressure, SpO₂ and cardiac output recorded continuously at ≥ 25 Hz.
  5. At 180 s the release manoeuvre of §4.3 is performed, then ventilation is resumed and a single recruitment manoeuvre given to the anaesthetist's usual practice. At any stopping criterion the tube is released and ventilation resumed immediately, with no measurement of any kind.
  6. Arterial blood gas at 5 and 10 minutes after resumption.

Stopping criteria — any one ends the sequence immediately

  • SpO₂ < 94%
  • Mean arterial pressure falls more than 20% below baseline, or below 60 mmHg
  • Any new arrhythmia, or heart rate outside 40–120 min⁻¹
  • Airway pressure more negative than −40 cmH₂O
  • Any clinical concern of the treating anaesthetist, at their sole discretion and without needing to give a reason
  • Request of the surgical team

The treating anaesthetist is not an investigator for the sequence and retains unconditional authority to stop it. An aborted sequence is recorded and analysed as far as it went; it is not a protocol violation and the participant is not replaced unless fewer than 60 s were obtained.

4.3 Measuring the entrained volume

The manoeuvre adds 15–20 seconds to the apnoea and is therefore permitted only when the sequence has run to 180 s with SpO₂ ≥ 96% and no stopping criterion met, and only with the treating anaesthetist's agreement at that moment. It is abandoned at once, and the patient ventilated, on any change. It is a secondary outcome and no participant is replaced for not having it.

Primary method — syringe restoration. With the tube still occluded and the transducer in line, gas of the same composition as the inspired mixture is injected from a calibrated large-volume syringe in steps until airway pressure returns to zero. The total injected volume, corrected as below, is the entrained volume.

  • Temperature and humidity. Syringe gas is dry at ambient temperature; the ATPD→BTPS factor at 21 °C is ≈ 1.12 and is applied to the injected volume. Ambient temperature and pressure are recorded for each participant.
  • Continued absorption. Gas is still being absorbed at ≈ 4 mL·s⁻¹ during the manoeuvre. Elapsed time from release of the first step to the zero-pressure endpoint is recorded and the absorbed volume added back.
  • Apparatus dead space. The volume of the syringe connector and tap is measured by water displacement once and subtracted.

This is the only method that measures pressure and volume on the same lung at the same instant. It is what Moreault could not do: their resorbed volumes and their airway pressures came from two separately randomised groups, so every volume–pressure pair in that dataset is a pair of cohort means rather than an observation.

Secondary method — flow integration. A wide-range ultrasonic flow meter at the tube, integrated from release to zero flow. Retained only as a cross-check, because the model predicts a peak flow of 7.8 L·s⁻¹ with 90% of the volume delivered within 0.20 s and flow below 1% of peak by 0.42 s, which is outside the linear range of a conventional heated pneumotachograph and whose added resistance would itself alter the quantity being measured. Transit-time ultrasonic devices are preferred because they respond to molar flow and are comparatively insensitive to gas composition, which changes during the inrush.

Regional distribution — electrical impedance tomography. Recorded continuously through the sequence and the release. EIT gives no absolute volume, but it answers the question the total volume cannot: whether the entrained gas enters dependent lung, which is recruitment, or only already-open non-dependent lung, which is not.

Optional extension — requires separate approval Injection continued beyond the zero-pressure endpoint in 100 mL steps, with a 3 s pause at each to read static pressure, yields a static inflation pressure–volume curve from the apnoeic point upward; the pressure at which compliance rises sharply is the opening pressure of the collapsed lung, which is the quantity that separates the model's 3–12 cmH₂O from Rothen's 30–40. This takes airway pressure above atmospheric in an apnoeic patient and is not part of the main protocol. It is described here so that it can be considered on its own merits, with its own risk assessment and its own consent, and it should not be approved as a rider to this study.

5 Risk and its mitigation

The central ethical argument is that this exposure already happens, unplanned and unmeasured, in routine practice. A ventilator disconnection, a period of apnoea during a difficult intubation, or an apnoea test are all the same physiological event. What this study adds is not the exposure but the measurement, a bounded duration, and stopping rules that ordinary practice does not have.

6.2 Analysis

  • Primary outcome summarised as mean with 95% confidence interval, and compared against each pre-specified prediction by one-sample t test. The registered predictions are the comparators; no post-hoc alternative is admitted.
  • Pressure trajectories modelled by mixed-effects regression with a random intercept per participant, testing for curvature — a plateau and a continuing fall are distinguishable by the sign of the quadratic term.
  • CO₂ slope by linear regression within participant, then summarised across.
  • Haemodynamic change from baseline by paired comparison at each time point.
  • Entrained volume summarised as mean with 95% confidence interval and compared against the registered 739 mL by one-sample t test. The recruitment test is one-sided in the direction of a shortfall, since the model has no mechanism that could produce a surplus; a surplus, if observed, is reported as a failure of the mass balance and not interpreted.
  • Coupling by within-subject correlation of pressure at 180 s against CO₂ slope, and of entrained volume against pressure — the two are predicted to be tightly related, and a dissociation between them localises the fault to the mechanics rather than the gas exchange.
  • Aborted sequences analysed to the point of abortion; no imputation.

7 Consent, data and governance

Written informed consent at the pre-operative visit, by an investigator, with adequate time for consideration. Participants are told what will be measured, that the exposure is a brief period of apnoea of the kind that occurs in routine anaesthesia, that it carries the risks set out above, that it will be stopped immediately on any concern, and that declining changes nothing about their care.

8 What the study settles

Each result is informative, including the ones unfavourable to the model that motivated it.

FindingInterpretation
Pressure at 180 s near −15 and still falling The thorax is drawn inward progressively. Collapse does not relieve the mechanical deficit, and models that hold the lung near its relaxation volume during obstruction are wrong.
Pressure plateaus near −10 Collapse is self-limiting. The model's lung mechanics are too stiff below residual volume, and its predictions of the state of the lung at cricothyroidotomy are too pessimistic.
CO₂ slope near 3.4 with either pressure result The model's CO₂ handling under obstruction is 26% too fast independently of its mechanics, and the fault is in gas transport rather than in the lung.
Cardiac output unchanged Confirms that the fall seen in animal work is hypoxic and autonomic rather than mechanical, and that the mechanical cardiac coupling in the model should be removed.
Entrained volume near 739 mL The gas balance is right: oxygen uptake, alveolar CO₂ delivery and nitrogen return are jointly correct under obstruction, and any error in the CO₂ result must then lie in the blood and tissue stores rather than at the alveolus.
Entrained volume short by 100–200 mL Lung that closed during the apnoea does not reopen at the pressures a passive inrush generates. Rothen's opening pressures apply in vivo, the model's recruitment limb is wrong, and the clinical implication — that relieving an obstruction does not restore the lung — is the most consequential result the study could produce.
Entrained volume short by more than 200 mL, or PaCO₂ and volume disagree The mass balance itself is wrong, not just its distribution. This would be the one outcome that invalidates the model rather than correcting it, and it is the reason the volume channel is worth the extra 20 seconds.
Pressure and CO₂ slope covary within subjects Identifies the coupling directly, and tells us which of the two limbs to correct — the observation no existing dataset can supply.
Predictions in §2.3 and §5 are generated from a computational model of apnoeic oxygenation and airway obstruction at a fixed, recorded version, and are registered as falsifiable predictions rather than offered as established physiology. The model is validated against 36 benchmarks including the Moreault, Stock and Chen & Scharf sources cited here, and its known disagreements with those sources are the reason for this study. The model's own documentation records that it cannot satisfy all three simultaneously.
